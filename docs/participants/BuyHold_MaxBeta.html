Post-mortem: why it worked, or why it did not

Mandate (as declared before the season)

@BuyHold_MaxBeta (Buy And Hold Max Beta) ran the "buy and hold (control)" archetype. Declared thesis: The control participant. Buy the three highest-beta names on the first session at 1.5x gross and never trade again. Everything else on the leaderboard is measured against what doing nothing but holding beta would have produced. Entry: On the first session, buy the top 3 names by declared beta at 50% of equity each. Exit: Never (except the mandatory liquidation at the end of the competition period). Sizing: 50% of equity per name = 150% gross. Leverage: 1.5x gross, static. Cadence: once, horizon all season. Aggression 3/5. This participant was built to maximise return, not to manage risk: Static high-beta exposure is the honest benchmark for 'highest return with no skill'.

Outcome (as measured after the season)

Outcome: +45.29% over 251 sessions - +45,287 USD on 100,000 of paper capital, ending at 145,287. Verdict: beat the market.

Why: the style premia that were actually live

The beta premium this participant is built to harvest was live (+39.73% over the season) and the account made money - a tailwind that cannot be separated from skill on one path. The market premium this participant is built to harvest was live (+14.42% over the season) and the account made money - a tailwind that cannot be separated from skill on one path.

Why: attribution of the return

Of the +45.29% total, about +45.76pp is explained by carrying beta 3.17 against a +14.41% market (R^2 0.31); the residual -0.47pp is everything else - selection, timing, leverage and cost. CVNA contributed +40,562 USD (90% of net P&L) across 1 closed trades; TSLA was the biggest drag at -64 USD.

Why: what execution cost

Execution cost 106 USD (0.11% of starting capital, 3.2 bp per fill, 3.1 bp of the 345,258 USD traded). 76% of that was spread/depth/impact and 24% was explicit fees and rebates.

Why: trade-level behaviour

3 closed round trips, 66.7% winners, profit factor 712.16, average winner +22,675 vs average loser -64 USD, median holding 250 sessions. Time in market, measured without double counting: 251 sessions held at least one position (the per-tranche holding periods above sum to 750 sessions because tranches of the same average-cost lot share its entry date, so they overlap and must not be added together). Single worst trade: TSLA long 2025-09-17 to 2026-09-16, -64 USD.

What the return-maximising mandate cost in drawdown

Worst peak-to-trough drawdown -31.97% (-9.10% for the index), trough on 2026-03-27, recovered 2026-04-16. Average gross exposure 184% of starting capital, peak leverage 1.64x. Daily returns were +0.01 skewed with excess kurtosis +0.14; historical 95% VaR -7.20%/day with CVaR -9.28%/day. Worst single session -12.74%, best +16.93%.

Regime context (real market data)

Season context: the real S&P 500 fell 9.10% from 2026-01-27 to 2026-03-30, VIX peaked at 31.05 on 2026-03-27, and cross-sectional dispersion was 35.8pp (stdev of individual 1-year returns).

Declared failure modes that fired

Declared failure mode(s) that actually fired: No ability to avoid a drawdown: the account takes the full market move times beta; Concentration in three declared-high-beta names.

Standing caveats: what this simulation cannot represent

The style premia quoted above are measured on the simulated replay, whose cross-sectional drifts are DECLARED scenario parameters (see sim/universe.py). They describe this competition, not the real market: IR-16.

Answer: did it work, and why

It beat the index by 30.87pp, but after beta the residual is -0.47pp: the win came from carrying more market risk (beta 3.17) in an up market, not from the signal itself. Finished at +45.29% against the S&P 500's +14.41% over the same 251 sessions - an excess return of +30.87pp.
