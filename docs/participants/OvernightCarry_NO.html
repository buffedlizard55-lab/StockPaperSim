Post-mortem: why it worked, or why it did not

Mandate (as declared before the season)

@OvernightCarry_NO (Overnight Carry) ran the "overnight premium" archetype. Declared thesis: A large share of the market's long-run return arrives overnight rather than during the session. This participant buys the index ETF at the close of every session and sells it at the open of the next, collecting the overnight leg only. Entry: Buy SPY at the close of every session (simulated as a market order at the next open with the overnight gap already realised); Hold only the index ETF; no single-name risk. Exit: Sell at the next session's open. Sizing: 2.0x gross overnight, flat during the session. Leverage: 2.0x gross overnight. Cadence: daily, horizon overnight. Aggression 3/5. This participant was built to maximise return, not to manage risk: Applies 2x leverage to the leg of the return distribution that the literature says is the paid one.

Outcome (as measured after the season)

Outcome: +14.11% over 251 sessions - +14,112 USD on 100,000 of paper capital, ending at 114,112. Verdict: roughly matched the market.

Why: the style premia that were actually live

The beta premium this participant is built to harvest was live (+39.73% over the season) and the account made money - a tailwind that cannot be separated from skill on one path.

Why: attribution of the return

Of the +14.11% total, about +15.14pp is explained by carrying beta 1.05 against a +14.41% market (R^2 0.49); the residual -1.03pp is everything else - selection, timing, leverage and cost. SPY contributed +12,941 USD (92% of net P&L) across 244 closed trades; SPY was the biggest drag at +12,941 USD.

Why: what execution cost

Execution cost 1,289 USD (1.29% of starting capital, 0.2 bp per fill, 0.2 bp of the 57,351,909 USD traded). 58% of that was spread/depth/impact and 42% was explicit fees and rebates.

Why: trade-level behaviour

244 closed round trips, 53.3% winners, profit factor 1.12, average winner +898 vs average loser -911 USD, median holding 1 sessions. Time in market, measured without double counting: 251 sessions held at least one position (the per-tranche holding periods above sum to 461 sessions because tranches of the same average-cost lot share its entry date, so they overlap and must not be added together). Single worst trade: SPY long 2026-06-01 to 2026-06-08, -5,337 USD.

What the return-maximising mandate cost in drawdown

Worst peak-to-trough drawdown -10.25% (-9.10% for the index), trough on 2026-09-16, never recovered. Average gross exposure 114% of starting capital, peak leverage 2.02x. Daily returns were +0.21 skewed with excess kurtosis +3.08; historical 95% VaR -1.81%/day with CVaR -2.60%/day. Worst single session -4.90%, best +5.83%.

Regime context (real market data)

Season context: the real S&P 500 fell 9.10% from 2026-01-27 to 2026-03-30, VIX peaked at 31.05 on 2026-03-27, and cross-sectional dispersion was 35.8pp (stdev of individual 1-year returns).

Declared failure modes that fired

None of the failure modes declared in the strategy spec fired in this window.

Standing caveats: what this simulation cannot represent

Standing limitations of this simulation that apply to this participant regardless of the regime: TIMING LIMITATION: in a daily-bar engine the overnight gap is already in the open price, so the entry is filled *after* the gap it is trying to capture. This structurally understates the strategy (flagged as IR-08). The style premia quoted above are measured on the simulated replay, whose cross-sectional drifts are DECLARED scenario parameters (see sim/universe.py). They describe this competition, not the real market: IR-16.

Answer: did it work, and why

It made money (+14.11%) but under-delivered versus simply holding the index (+14.41%); the signal did not pay for its own costs, drawdown and turnover. Finished at +14.11% versus the S&P 500's +14.41% - effectively in line with simply holding the index.
