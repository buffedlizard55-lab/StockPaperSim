Post-mortem: why it worked, or why it did not

Mandate (as declared before the season)

@PairsArb_ZScore2 (Pairs Stat Arb) ran the "statistical arbitrage" archetype. Declared thesis: Two names in the same sector move together. When their price ratio stretches two standard deviations from its 60-session mean, short the relative winner and buy the relative loser and wait for convergence. Entry: Find the pair with the highest 60-session return correlation inside the same sector; Compute the z-score of log(price_A) - beta * log(price_B) over 60 sessions; Enter when |z| >= 2.0, dollar-neutral, 1.0x gross each leg. Exit: Exit at |z| <= 0.25; Exit unconditionally after 20 sessions. Sizing: dollar neutral, 100% long + 100% short = 200% gross. Leverage: 2.0x gross, ~0 net. Cadence: daily, horizon 1-20 sessions. Aggression 3/5. This participant was built to maximise return, not to manage risk: Leverage, not diversification: a market-neutral book at 2x gross can out-return a long-only book if convergence is fast.

Outcome (as measured after the season)

Outcome: +1.01% over 251 sessions - +1,005 USD on 100,000 of paper capital, ending at 101,005. Verdict: made money but lagged the index.

Why: the style premia that were actually live

The reversal premium went against the declared exposure (-6.01% for a +0.5 loading) yet the account still returned +1.01% - the result was driven by something other than this factor. The low_vol premium went against the declared exposure (-21.86% for a +0.5 loading) yet the account still returned +1.01% - the result was driven by something other than this factor.

Why: attribution of the return

Of the +1.01% total, about -0.11pp is explained by carrying beta -0.01 against a +14.41% market (R^2 0.01); the residual +1.11pp is everything else - selection, timing, leverage and cost. SPY contributed +1,451 USD (144% of net P&L) across 2 closed trades; QQQ was the biggest drag at -411 USD.

Why: what execution cost

Execution cost 7 USD (0.01% of starting capital, 0.2 bp per fill, 0.2 bp of the 400,248 USD traded). 72% of that was spread/depth/impact and 28% was explicit fees and rebates.

Why: trade-level behaviour

3 closed round trips, 66.7% winners, profit factor 3.54, average winner +726 vs average loser -411 USD, median holding 20 sessions. Time in market, measured without double counting: 178 sessions held at least one position (the per-tranche holding periods above sum to 217 sessions because tranches of the same average-cost lot share its entry date, so they overlap and must not be added together). Single worst trade: QQQ short 2025-12-31 to 2026-01-30, -411 USD.

What the return-maximising mandate cost in drawdown

Worst peak-to-trough drawdown -0.37% (-9.10% for the index), trough on 2026-01-27, recovered 2026-01-30. Average gross exposure 16% of starting capital, peak leverage 2.00x. Daily returns were +9.63 skewed with excess kurtosis +109.20; historical 95% VaR -0.01%/day with CVaR -0.06%/day. Worst single session -0.15%, best +0.81%.

Regime context (real market data)

Season context: the real S&P 500 fell 9.10% from 2026-01-27 to 2026-03-30, VIX peaked at 31.05 on 2026-03-27, and cross-sectional dispersion was 35.8pp (stdev of individual 1-year returns).

Standing caveats: what this simulation cannot represent

The style premia quoted above are measured on the simulated replay, whose cross-sectional drifts are DECLARED scenario parameters (see sim/universe.py). They describe this competition, not the real market: IR-16.

Answer: did it work, and why

It made money (+1.01%) but under-delivered versus simply holding the index (+14.41%); the signal did not pay for its own costs, drawdown and turnover. Finished at +1.01%, profitable but 13.41pp behind the S&P 500's +14.41%.

Why return-seeking. Leverage, not diversification: a market-neutral book at 2x gross can out-return a long-only book if convergence is fast.

Exit
